# Guidance

How to make a combination and how to use one: first in Wada's words, from the booklets he wrote for his combinations, then in Hama Yaohiko's, from the colour atlas they made together.

## Wada's advice {#wada}

Wada didn't think combining colours could be taught like mathematics, where a formula gives the right answer: a combination always appeals to each person's feelings and taste. Taste in colour, he said, is like a singing voice, partly a gift, but it grows with practice. So he gave advice from what he had learnt as a painter: rules of thumb, not a science.

### Making a combination {#making}

- **Balance lightness and saturation, not only hue.** Lining up well-chosen hues isn't enough: a combination works through how its colours' lightness and saturation balance one another.
  - Vivid colours together need a muted one among them or beside them.
  - Pale colours together feel lukewarm: they need a deep one, in a measured amount.
  - Dark colours together are suffocating: some of them must be lighter.

  As in cooking, he said: a meal comes together through the balance of hot, sweet and sour.
- **Don't make every colour as strong as the others.** When no colour sets off another, the result is safe, but ordinary (see [his first card](#guidance/cards)).
- **Start from one hue,** in its light and dark tones: it can hardly go wrong. Set different hues against each other with care.
- **Where different hues touch,** weigh each colour's lightness and strength, or the result turns coarse.
- **Calm colours for things you live with.** Strong combinations catch the eye, which suits advertising, shop windows and the stage, but they tire quickly. Calm doesn't mean dull: neighbouring hues, their strengths well balanced.

### Four kinds of harmony {#harmony}

1. **Like colours:** one hue in its light and dark tones, such as orange with its browns and its pale, flesh-like tints. Gentle and safe, but seldom more than ordinary. The place to start.
2. **Different hues:** colours of quite different hues, in any tone. Interesting, but it needs care (above).
3. **Complementary:** opposite hues, whatever their lightness. Lively, but suited to fewer uses.
4. **Graded bands** (*ungen*, 暈繝): one hue in steps, from white through the full colour to black, or part of that scale. The calmest of all.

An earlier version has two kinds, same tone and different tone, each light, dark, pale, deep or free: ten in all. He called them a law and a standard, but not one you must obey: those with long experience of colour can merge them or break them. None of his plates says which kind it is.

### His complementary pairs {#pairs}

Wada paired colours of his own palette by hue alone, so their lightness and saturation vary. Red's lighter and darker tones pair too: [[Hermosa Pink]] and [[Pale Burnt Lake]] go with [[Sea Green]], as [[Spectrum Red]] does.

::: pairs
- [[Spectrum Red]] ↔ [[Sea Green]]
- [[Red Orange]] ↔ [[Green Blue]], or one a little greener
- [[Orange]] ↔ [[Peacock Blue]]
- [[Yellow Orange]] ↔ [[Antwerp Blue]]
- [[Apricot Yellow]] ↔ [[Blue]]
- [[Yellow]] ↔ [[Deep Lyons Blue]]
- [[Lemon Yellow]] ↔ [[Violet Ultramarine]]
- [[Light Green Yellow]] ↔ [[Violet]]
- [[Yellow Green]] ↔ [[Red Violet]]
- [[Night Green]] ↔ [[Rosolane Purple]]
:::

Only one of these pairs is a combination in the book: [022](#combination/22), Yellow with Deep Lyons Blue.

### White, grey or black between colours {#neutrals}

Where two colours clash, put white, grey or black between them. Wada passed on the rules of the French chemist Michel-Eugène Chevreul, whom he called the leading authority on colour contrast and harmony, on which to use:

- red and yellow: anything but black; red and blue: anything but white; blue and yellow: anything but grey; yellow and orange: anything but black; blue and purple: anything but grey;
- orange and green: black; purples: grey; purple and green: white;
- black never clashes with two bright colours, and suits dark purples and blues; grey suits two bright colours, and never clashes with two dull ones;
- on a pale ground, draw patterns in deep tones, outlined in black, grey or a colour deeper still; a pattern paler than its ground takes a white or grey outline.

But taste moves on, Wada added. By Chevreul's rules red with blue is pleasing, yet few would now agree; brown beside black, long frowned on, is now well liked.

### Using a combination {#using}

- **Give the colours different areas.** The plates show every colour the same size only for convenience. Equal areas balance only when the colours are equally strong: otherwise, give the weak tones the large areas and the strong ones the small.
- **Try the amounts.** Lay cut white or grey paper over a plate, to change each colour's shape and size. For a kimono, a triangle: its tip is the collar, the middle the kimono, the next part the obi.
- **The main colour can be a small one.** On a card, the largest colour is the main one, holding the rest together; in actual dress, a small one often takes that role: the obi, the collar, a hat, gloves, a handbag. Where is the centre of the combination? That is the question.
- **Large areas look paler.** A combination that works in small swatches looks paler and weaker on a large object: clothes ordered from swatches can come out looking washed out.

### Wada reads two of his cards {#cards}

He explained two of his seasonal cards, both for January. On each, the top colour is the largest.

::: card season=1
[[Seashell Pink]] with [[White]], [[Sulphur Yellow]], [[Glaucous Green]] and [[Grayish Lavender - A]].

All are light and, apart from the white, about as strong as one another, with little difference in hue, lightness or saturation: none helps or sets off another, each just asserts itself. Ordinary, he said, though safe: the kind anyone makes.
:::

::: card season=5
[[Black]] with [[Spectrum Red]], [[Ivory Buff]], [[Dark Tyrian Blue]], [[Cobalt Green]] and [[Warm Gray]].

Different in every way, and uneven in strength, yet natural, and full of change:

- the black, darkest of colours, holds the others together;
- the red, a pure colour, would look coarse alone: the black's embrace and the Ivory Buff below it soften it;
- with the weak Cobalt Green below, the Dark Tyrian Blue tightens the whole;
- the grey, last, changes none of the others.

The black covers the most, but the eye goes first to the red: here, the red is the centre.
:::

## Hama's advice {#hama}

::: aside
This part is Hama Yaohiko's, not Wada's: from his commentary to the colour atlas they made together. Hama had written an outline of colour theory before it, and the atlas's publisher called him an authority on colour.
:::

He treats combining colours only in outline, and sets no absolute laws: what we like depends on custom, age, upbringing and surroundings, and fashion changes it.

- **Good and poor pairs.** Tests that averaged many people's judgements agree: complementary pairs, or pairs a little off them, are good; a colour with the hue halfway to its complement is poor.
  - Good: red and blue; red and blue-green; orange and green-blue or blue; yellow and violet; yellow and blue; yellow-green and violet; green and red-purple. (Wada, by contrast, doubted that red with blue still pleased.)
  - Poor: red and orange; red and yellow; orange and yellow; yellow and green or blue-green; yellow-green and orange or green-blue; green and blue.
- **Three or more colours.** Take hues that divide the hue circle about equally, such as red, yellow and blue, and make one or two of them lighter or darker.
- **Same and similar colours** (one colour, deep and pale, light and dark, or neighbouring hues) are generally liked as mild and pleasant.
- **Variety and unity.** Opposite colours give variety, and look beautiful at first, but tire with long looking: people come to prefer the plainer, milder combinations they first thought dull. So combining colours is balancing variety and unity, for the purpose in hand. Where there is too much variety, change some colours' lightness and saturation to unify them.
- **One dominant colour.** Many colours together easily look scattered: let one of them dominate, to hold the rest together.
- **Tone, like a key in music.** Colours of one tone (equally light, pale or greyed) sit together; different tones, used well, add life and save a design from flatness; used thoughtlessly, they are off-key.
- **Even steps.** Within one series of tints, shades or greyed colours, colours at even steps, every second or every third, go well together.
- **Seen from afar.** In earlier tests he cites, black on yellow read from furthest away, then green, red and blue on white; in a more recent one, lightness contrast decided most of it.
