# Colour theory

The ideas behind the book and this site's views: how colour works, how it is described, and what changes it, as Wada and Hama taught them in the 1930s. Where today's colour science says otherwise, a note says so.

## Light and colour {#light}

White light is a mix of wavelengths: a prism spreads it into the spectrum, from red to violet. An object has a colour because it absorbs part of the light and sends back the rest: its colour is white light, less what it absorbs, and in the dark it has none. A red paint doesn't send back only red light, Hama pointed out: a pigment absorbs about half the spectrum, and one that sent back only red would look almost black.

> **Today:** the eye's three kinds of cone, which Hama doubted (none had been found), are established. The brain makes the colour from them, so the same light can look different in different surroundings, and different lights can look the same.

## Describing a colour {#attributes}

Wada and Hama both describe a colour by three attributes:

- **Hue:** red, yellow, green, blue and so on, round a circle that closes through the purples, which the spectrum lacks.
- **Lightness:** how light or dark it is, matched against a scale of greys.
- **Saturation:** how strong the colour is, against the pure one. Wada's earlier booklet treats it as depth: red thinned with water is paler, red laid over red deeper. Hama keeps depth apart: a colour laid over itself takes on black, and grows darker.

Adding white to a colour gives tints, adding black gives shades, adding grey gives greyed (broken) tones: from one hue, hundreds of colours. Hama drew them as a triangle, after Ostwald, with the full colour at one corner and white and black at the others; all the hues' triangles together make a solid, white at the top, black at the bottom, the full colours round the middle. He knew this was simplified: yellow's full colour should sit higher, and blue's lower.

Full colours aren't equally light. Matched against greys, yellow is lightest, then orange-yellow and yellow-green, then orange and green, red and blue-green, blue and red-purple, and purple darkest.

Nor do equal amounts of white look like equal steps: a little white lightens a dark grey far more than a light one. So Hama made his grey scale with its white falling by a constant ratio, not in tenths.

> **Today:** most colour systems still use these three attributes, now measured so that equal steps look equal (see [colour systems](#theory/systems)), and the measured solid is lopsided, yellow's full colour far lighter than blue's. Greys that look evenly spaced follow roughly the cube root of how much light they send back (Munsell's value, CIE L*): a constant ratio crowds the dark steps.

## Complementary colours {#complements}

Two colours are complementary when, mixed as light or spun together on a disc, they make white or grey. Hama called them twins: the light a colour absorbs is its complement. The eye makes them too: stare at a red paper, take it away, and a blue-green after-image appears in its place. Wada paired colours of his own palette: see [his pairs](#guidance/pairs).

> **Today:** which colours are complements depends on the model. The painters' pairs (red and green, orange and blue, yellow and purple) differ from those of light (red and cyan, green and magenta, blue and yellow); Wada's and Hama's pairs, such as yellow and blue, or red and blue-green, are close to light's. After-images come from the eye adapting to a colour.

## Mixing colours {#mixing}

- **Paints subtract.** Each absorbs part of the light, so a mix is darker than its colours, with a hue between theirs on the circle: the further apart they are, the duller the mix, and opposite colours make grey or near black. Painters and dyers often dull a colour with its complement rather than with black, for a softer result.
- **Light adds.** Red, green and blue lights make every hue: red and green make yellow, and all three make white. Spun on a disc, as Hama mixed them, or in fine dots, colours average instead: a complementary pair gives grey, not white.
- **Yellow and blue paint make green,** not grey: between them they absorb both ends of the spectrum, and leave its middle.

> **Today:** the painters' red, yellow and blue are a tradition, not a law. Printing mixes cyan, magenta and yellow (Hama's advice, to choose a greenish blue and a purplish red, already leads that way), and screens mix red, green and blue. Rules of proportion such as George Field's, 3 of yellow to 5 of red and 8 of blue, depend on the paints.

## Contrast {#contrast}

- **Side by side,** colours push each other apart: each looks tinged with the other's complement. Close hues shift most, and complements keep their hue. Larger, more saturated and lighter areas push hardest.
- **Lightness:** a colour looks lighter beside a darker one, and darker beside a lighter one. White looks whitest on black.
- **Saturation:** a pale colour beside a vivid one looks paler still.
- **One after another:** a colour seen just after its complement looks more vivid.
- **A grey on a coloured ground** looks tinged with the ground's complement.
- **A line stops it:** a black outline, a gap or an overlap between two colours removes the effect, which may be one reason [white, grey or black between colours](#guidance/neutrals) works.
- **From afar, small colours mix:** fine stripes or dots blend into their average. Japanese textiles use it, in *komon*, *kasuri* and stripes: separate colours near, a mixture far, and in between a beauty of its own.

## What changes a colour {#conditions}

- **The light.** Lamplight is rich in orange and poor in blue: reds and oranges glow, yellow fades, blues darken. Hama found colour hard to judge by lamplight. Strong sun favours reds and yellows, dull weather pale, cool colours.
- **Dim light.** At dusk, red goes dark first, while greens and blues still show their colour.
- **The material.** The same colour differs as oil paint, watercolour or dye, and on silk, cotton or linen: oils look dark and deep, pastels light, watercolours between. A wet surface looks more saturated than a dry one.
- **The area.** Large areas look paler and weaker than small swatches, as [Wada warned](#guidance/using).

> **Today:** the eye also adapts to the light, which hides much of its shift; and two colours that match in one light can differ in another.

## Strong and weak, warm and cool {#character}

- **Strength.** Wada ranked colours by how much they excite the eye: red most, then orange, yellow, green, blue and indigo, violet least. That is why danger signals are red. People differ, he said, but this order is generally accepted.
- **Warm and cool.** Red, orange and yellow are warm, blue and green cool: Hama traced it to fire and to deep, clear water. But every hue has warmer and cooler kinds: for Wada, celadon is a warm green and evergreen a cool one, and canary a cooler yellow than turmeric.
- **What colours stand for,** in Hama's list, "a kind of code": white and black, purity and seriousness; red, power and passion; yellow, light and joy; green, peace and health; blue, calm and depth; purple, grace and nobility.

> **Today:** no fixed order of strength by hue is established: how much a colour stands out depends on its saturation, lightness and surroundings, and red for danger is partly convention.

## Colour systems {#systems}

A colour system orders colours by their attributes, so that any colour can be found and written down. This site places Wada's colours in four, each a view under [Colors](#colors):

- [**Ostwald**](#colors/ostwald): every colour a mix of full colour, white and black, on a circle of 100 hues, each hue a triangle, as Hama drew it. Wada wrote colours in it: his orange is 16PA, his dark brown 17PK.
- [**OKLCH**](#colors/oklch): a modern colour space, of lightness, chroma and hue, made so that equal steps look equal. The site computes every colour in it, from the book's CMYK, and uses it to show them on screen.
- [**Munsell**](#colors/munsell): hue, value (lightness) and chroma, spaced to look even, chroma reaching as far as colours go. Japan's colour standards write colours in it.
- [**PCCS**](#colors/pccs): made by the Japan Color Research Institute, which grew out of the association Wada founded. Built on Munsell's notation, it adds tones (colours of like lightness and saturation across the hues), for colour harmony.

## Colour names {#names}

Wada gathered colour names into books, and gave every colour of his combinations a Japanese name and an English one. The English names come from Robert Ridgway's *Color Standards and Color Nomenclature*, a few misspelled. Plate 8 of the atlas shows 12 hues, each as tint, full colour and shade, with Wada's names: 35 of them are colours here, and those colours' dialogs say so.

Hama adds notes on Japanese names: *ao* long meant both green and blue; the browns (*cha*) and the mouse greys (*nezumi*) were the two great families of colour names in the Edo period; and Kyoto's purple was redder than Edo's.
