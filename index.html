Guidance

Where the advice comes from

Wada's book, the Haishoku Sōkan (1933–34), shows 348 combinations of two, three and four colours, every colour at the same size. Wada also made 72 seasonal combinations (1934–35), six for each month, as cards: on each, one large colour on top and smaller bands below.

He wrote two booklets on how to use them: one in 1934, sent out with the book (today's reprint, A Dictionary of Color Combinations, has part of it), and a longer supplement in 1935. Wada's advice below comes from them. In 1935 he also published a colour atlas with Hama Yaohiko, and Hama's advice, at the end, comes from his commentary to it.

Wada didn't say which of his combinations show which advice. The examples here are combinations from the book that show each point, chosen for this site by their colours' lightness, saturation and hue. Each opens its page.

The ideas the advice builds on, such as hue, lightness, saturation and complementary colours, are explained in Colour theory.

Wada's advice

Wada didn't think combining colours could be taught like mathematics, where a formula gives the right answer: a combination always appeals to each person's feelings and taste. Taste in colour, he said, is like a singing voice, partly a gift, but it grows with practice. So he gave advice from what he had learnt as a painter: rules of thumb, not a science.

Making a combination

  • Balance lightness and saturation, not only hue. Lining up well-chosen hues isn't enough: a combination works through how its colours' lightness and saturation balance one another.
    • Vivid colours together need a muted one among them or beside them.
    • Pale colours together feel lukewarm: they need a deep one, in a measured amount.
    • Dark colours together are suffocating: some of them must be lighter.
    As in cooking, he said: a meal comes together through the balance of hot, sweet and sour.

    • Vivid Benzol Green and Jasper Red, with a muted Deep Indigo.

    • Three pale colours, with a deep Brick Red.

    • Two dark colours, Dull Violet Black and Brown, with a lighter Citron Yellow.

  • Don't make every colour as strong as the others. When no colour sets off another, the result is safe, but ordinary (see his first card).

    • Three pale colours, about as strong as one another.

  • Start from one hue, in its light and dark tones: it can hardly go wrong. Set different hues against each other with care.
  • Where different hues touch, weigh each colour's lightness and strength, or the result turns coarse.
  • Calm colours for things you live with. Strong combinations catch the eye, which suits advertising, shop windows and the stage, but they tire quickly. Calm doesn't mean dull: neighbouring hues, their strengths well balanced.

Four kinds of harmony

  1. Like colours. One hue in its light and dark tones, such as orange with its browns and its pale, flesh-like tints. Gentle and safe, but seldom more than ordinary. The place to start.
  2. Different hues. Colours of quite different hues, in any tone. Interesting, but it needs care (above).
  3. Complementary. Opposite hues, whatever their lightness. Lively, but suited to fewer uses.
  4. Graded bands (ungen, 暈繝): one hue in steps, from white through the full colour to black, or part of that scale. The calmest of all.

  • Like colours, one red from the deep Vistoris Lake to the pale Cameo Pink.

  • Different hues, Antwerp Blue, Red Violet and Cinnamon Rufous.

  • Complementary, Eugenia Red and Sea Green, close to his own pair of red and Sea Green (below).

His 1934 booklet sorts them differently, into two kinds, same tone and different tone, each light, dark, pale, deep or free: ten in all. He called them a law and a standard, but not one you must obey: those with long experience of colour can merge them or break them. None of the book's combinations says which kind it is.

White, grey or black between colours

Where two colours clash, put white, grey or black between them. Wada passed on the rules of the French chemist Michel-Eugène Chevreul, whom he called the leading authority on colour contrast and harmony, on which to use:

  • Red and yellow. White or grey, not black.
  • Red and blue. Grey or black, not white.
  • Blue and yellow. White or black, not grey.
  • Yellow and orange. White or grey, not black.
  • Blue and purple. White or black, not grey.
  • Orange and green. Black.
  • Purples. Grey.
  • Purple and green. White.
  • Black never clashes with two bright colours, and suits dark purples and blues. Grey suits two bright colours, and never clashes with two dull ones.
  • Outlines. On a pale ground, draw patterns in deep tones, outlined in black, grey or a colour deeper still. A pattern paler than its ground takes a white or grey outline.

  • Grey between Carmine Red and Pale Lemon Yellow.

  • Black between Jasper Red and Green.

  • Grey between Light Mauve and Cinnamon Buff.

But taste moves on, Wada added. By Chevreul's rules red with blue is pleasing, yet few would now agree. Brown beside black, long frowned on, is now well liked.

Using a combination

  • Give the colours different areas. The book shows every colour the same size only for convenience. Equal areas balance only when the colours are equally strong: otherwise, give the weak tones the large areas and the strong ones the small.

    • A weak Light Glaucous Blue on the large area, a strong Red on a small one.

  • Try the amounts. Lay cut white or grey paper over a combination, to change each colour's shape and size: the book came with cut-out cards for it, a triangle, a rectangle and a wavy band. For a kimono, the triangle: its tip is the collar, the middle the kimono, the next part the obi.
  • The main colour can be a small one. On the seasonal cards, the largest colour, on top, is the main one, holding the rest together. In actual dress, a small one often takes that role: the obi, the collar, a hat, gloves, a handbag. Where is the centre of the combination? That is the question (see his second card).
  • Large areas look paler. A combination that works in small swatches looks paler and weaker on a large object: clothes ordered from swatches can come out looking washed out.

Hama's advice

This part is Hama Yaohiko's, not Wada's: from his commentary to the colour atlas they made together. Hama had written an outline of colour theory before it, and the atlas's publisher called him an authority on colour.

He treats combining colours only in outline, and sets no absolute laws: what we like depends on custom, age, upbringing and surroundings, and fashion changes it.

  • Good and poor pairs. Tests that averaged many people's judgements agree: complementary pairs, or pairs a little off them, are good. A colour with the hue halfway to its complement is poor.
    • Good: red and blue, red and blue-green, orange and green-blue (or blue), yellow and violet, yellow and blue, yellow-green and violet, green and red-purple. (Wada, by contrast, doubted that red with blue still pleased.)
    • Poor: red and orange, red and yellow, orange and yellow, yellow and green (or blue-green), yellow-green and orange (or green-blue), green and blue.
  • Three or more colours. Take hues that divide the hue circle about equally, such as red, yellow and blue, and make one or two of them lighter or darker.

    • A blue, a yellow and a purplish red, about a third of the circle apart, the blue dark and the yellow light.

  • Same and similar colours (one colour, deep and pale, light and dark, or neighbouring hues) are generally liked as mild and pleasant.

    • One red, deep and pale, Pompeian Red and Cameo Pink.

  • Variety and unity. Opposite colours give variety, and look beautiful at first, but tire with long looking: people come to prefer the plainer, milder combinations they first thought dull. So combining colours is balancing variety and unity, for the purpose in hand. Where there is too much variety, change some colours' lightness and saturation to unify them.
  • One dominant colour. Many colours together easily look scattered: let one of them dominate, to hold the rest together.
  • Tone, like a key in music. Colours of one tone (equally light, pale or greyed) sit together. Different tones, used well, add life and save a design from flatness. Used thoughtlessly, they are off-key.

    • Eosine Pink, Orange Yellow and Citron Yellow, of about the same lightness and saturation.

  • Even steps. Within one series of tints, shades or greyed colours, colours at even steps, every second or every third, go well together.
  • Seen from afar. In earlier tests he cites, black on yellow read from furthest away, then green, red and blue on white. In a more recent one, lightness contrast decided most of it.

Colour theory

Before combining colours, it helps to know what a colour is made of. This page is a short introduction to colour theory: what colour is, how to describe it, how colours mix and act on one another, and how they are ordered and named.

The aim is to help you understand Wada Sanzō's work: the basics of colour as he understood them, from the booklets that went with his combinations (1934 and 1935), and the system and names he wrote his colours in. Hama Yaohiko, who made a colour atlas with him in 1935, covered the same ground in more detail in its commentary, and fills in much of what follows. Where he saw something differently, the text says so. Colour science has moved on since the 1930s, so where it now says otherwise, a note explains what changed. The site also adds three other colour systems to Ostwald's.

You don't need any of it to use the combinations, but it explains the words Guidance uses and how the Colors views order the palette.

Light and colour

Colour needs light. In the dark, nothing has a colour. White light, such as sunlight, is a mix of all the colours, and a prism spreads it out into the spectrum, from red to violet.

An object absorbs part of the light that falls on it and reflects the rest, and the light it reflects is what we see as its colour. A pigment absorbs about half the spectrum and reflects the half around its own colour. A red that reflected only red light would look almost black, not bright red, because it would reflect so little light.

An object that absorbed all light would look black, and one that reflected all of it would look white. White, black and the greys between them are colours without hue. No material is perfectly white or perfectly black, so whites differ from one another, and so do blacks, just as other colours do.

The light itself isn't coloured, though. The colour is made in the eye and the brain. In the eye, cones see colour in daylight, and rods, which see only light and dark, take over in dim light.

So a colour is never quite fixed. It changes with the light, with the colours around it and with the material it's on. That is true of this site too, as Wada's colours are printed inks, which reflect light, while a screen gives off light of its own. The colours here are computed from the books' printing values. They come close to the printed page, but every screen shows them a little differently.

Describing a colour

Wada and Hama both describe a colour by three attributes:

  • Hue. The quality we call red, yellow, green, blue and so on, set by the part of the spectrum a colour reflects most. The purples, which mix the spectrum's two ends, red and violet, join those ends, so the hues run on with no start or end, and are often drawn as a circle.
  • Lightness. How light or dark it is, matched against a scale of greys.
  • Saturation. How strong the colour is, against the pure one. Wada's 1934 booklet treats it as depth, so red thinned with water is paler, and red laid over red deeper. Hama keeps depth apart, as a colour laid over itself takes on black and grows darker. Many colour systems today use a close relative, chroma, which measures how far a colour is from the grey of the same lightness.

Tone brings the last two together. Colours of different hues with about the same lightness and saturation, all pale, say, or all dark and greyed, share a tone. Much of the advice in Guidance is about tones.

Mixing a colour with white gives its tints, with black its shades, and with grey its greyed tones, so one hue gives hundreds of colours. Ostwald, whose system Wada used, drew them as a triangle, with the full colour at one corner and white and black at the other two. Stood round an axis of greys, the triangles of all the hues make a three-dimensional shape, two cones joined at their bases. This colour solid has white at the top, black at the bottom and the full colours round its widest part, the middle (see the Ostwald view).

Full colours aren't equally light, though. Matched against greys, yellow is the lightest, then orange-yellow and yellow-green, then orange and green, red and blue-green, blue and red-purple, and purple is the darkest.

Hama's view: in the colour solid, the higher a colour sits, the lighter it is, yet every full colour sits on the same ring round the middle, as if all were equally light. Hama noted that this simplifies things. Yellow's full colour is much lighter than blue's, so it should sit higher, nearer the white, and blue's lower, nearer the black.

Equal amounts of white don't give equal steps of lightness either. A little white lightens a dark grey far more than a light one, so the grey scale behind the system lets its white fall by a constant ratio, not in tenths.

Since then: many systems, such as Munsell's, PCCS and OKLCH, space lightness in steps that look equal (see Colour systems), so in their colour solids the full colours sit at different heights, as Hama said they should. Not all do, and the Swedish NCS, for one, keeps Ostwald's approach.

Complementary colours

Two colours are complementary when, mixed as light or spun together on a disc, they make white or grey. They come in pairs because of how a colour is made. A pigment absorbs part of white light and reflects the rest, and the part it absorbs, seen as light on its own, is the complement of its colour. Together the two make up white light again, like twins born of the same light.

The eye makes complements too. Stare for a while at a red paper on a grey ground, then take it away, and a blue-green after-image appears in its place.

On a hue circle, a colour's complement lies roughly opposite it. Ostwald built his so that it always does. To find a colour's exact complement, spin it together with another on a disc, changing how much of each shows. Complements give a neutral grey, and any other pair leaves a trace of colour.

* The discs blur as they spin, as the eye blurs a real one. A screen shows the spin frame by frame, so on its own it blends the colours only partly.

Many painting books pair colours differently, from a circle built on red, yellow and blue paint. Each of the three is paired with the mix of the other two, so red with green, yellow with purple and blue with orange. Those pairs come from mixing paint, and paint doesn't mix like light. Yellow and blue paint make green (see Mixing colours), while yellow and blue light make white. The pairs here are those of light.

A complement is a matter of hue alone, so a colour's lighter and darker tones share it. Pink and maroon have the same complement as red. Wada drew up a table of such pairs from his own palette (see his pairs).

Mixing colours

Colours can be mixed in three ways, and each gives different results.

  • Paints subtract. Each paint absorbs part of the light, and in a mix each also absorbs some of what the other reflects. As a rule of thumb, the mix's hue lies between the two on the hue circle, and it's darker than the two averaged. The further apart they are, the duller and darker the mix, until opposite colours give grey or near black. That's why painters often dull a colour with a touch of its complement rather than with black, for a softer result.

    Some pairs break the rule, because a mix depends on all the light each paint reflects, not only on how it looks. The best known is yellow and blue. Mixed as light, they make white, as complements do. Mixed as paints, they make green. Yellow paint absorbs the blue end of the spectrum and blue paint the red end, and the green in the middle, which both reflect, is all that's left.

  • Light adds. Lights shone together add up, so a mix is lighter than either. Red and green light make yellow. The further apart two lights are on the circle, the paler the mix, and opposite colours make white.
  • Spinning and dots average. On a spinning disc, or in dots too small to see apart, colours neither subtract nor add but average. The mix is as light as the two averaged, so a complementary pair gives grey, not white. Japanese textiles have long used this, in komon, kasuri and stripes. Up close they show separate colours, from afar a mixture, and in between a beauty of their own.

Each kind of mixing starts from a few colours, its primaries, chosen so that their mixes give as many colours as possible. For light they are red, green and blue. For paints, long practice settled on red, yellow and blue.

With paints, which red, yellow and blue you choose matters. The most colours come from a yellow leaning to neither orange nor green, a slightly greenish blue such as Prussian blue, and a purplish red such as carmine. Even so, no choice suits every mix. Primaries that give a clear green give dull purples, and the other way round.

Since then: the primaries for paints and inks are taken to be cyan, magenta and yellow, close to the greenish blue and purplish red above, and red, yellow and blue are seen as a painters' tradition. No three primaries, of paint or of light, mix every colour either. They give every hue, but not every hue at its strongest.

Contrast

A colour doesn't look the same everywhere. What lies beside it, and what was seen just before, changes how it looks. This is contrast.

Side by side, two colours push each other apart, in three ways that often appear together.

  • Hue. Each colour looks tinged with the other's complement, so the two seem further apart on the hue circle than they are. The closer the two hues, the bigger the shift. Complements don't shift at all, and each shows its own hue at its fullest. A grey on a coloured ground takes on a tinge of the ground's complement.

  • Lightness. A colour looks lighter beside a darker one, and darker beside a lighter one. White looks whitest on black.

  • Saturation. A pale colour beside a vivid one looks paler still.

The larger, more saturated or lighter colour pushes harder, and two equal ones push each other alike. It happens where two colours meet on one surface with nothing marking the edge. An outline, a gap or an overlap between them stops it, which may be one reason white, grey or black between colours works.

One after another, colours change too, because the after-image of the first, its complement, mixes into the next. A colour seen just after its complement looks more vivid. Seen after the same or a close hue, it looks duller, and a close hue also pushes it away, so orange seen after red looks yellower.

What changes a colour

A colour isn't fixed in the thing that carries it. The same paint looks different in another light, on another material, at another size, and as it ages.

  • The light. Daylight comes from two places, the sun and the sky. On its way through the air, sunlight loses some of its blue, which the air scatters across the sky. So direct sun is orange-yellow, the sky is blue, and together they make white. Strong sun favours reds, oranges and yellows and dulls blues and purples. Quince, pomegranate and marigold blaze at noon, while wisteria, iris and hydrangea are at their best in rain, haze and the soft light of morning and evening.

    Lamplight depends on the lamp, but it's usually richer in orange and poorer in blue than daylight. Under it, reds and oranges glow, yellow turns whitish, green leans to yellow-green, blues darken and purple turns redder, so colour is hard to judge. A coloured light goes further. Colours of its own hue look white, and its complement grey or black.

    Since then: the eye adapts to the light more than these lists suggest. After a few minutes under lamplight it discounts much of the light's colour, so the shifts show most at first, or beside daylight. Two colours that match in one light can still differ in another.

  • Dim light. At dusk, red goes dark first and yellow loses its colour, while greens and blues still show theirs. In faint light the eye's cones, which see colour, gradually stop working, and its rods take over. Rods see only light and dark, and they respond most to blue-green, so blues and greens keep their lightness, while reds go almost black.

  • The material. Part of the light bounces off a surface as white before it reaches the colour, and the more it does, the whiter the colour looks. Water fills a rough surface and cuts this down, so a colour looks deeper wet than dry. For the same reason, pastels look light, oils dark and deep, and watercolours in between.

    What the colour sits on matters too. The same dye looks different on silk, cotton or hemp, because their fibres reflect light differently. A colour thinned with water is transparent and lets the ground show through, while one thinned with white is opaque and hides it.

  • The area. Wada warned that large areas look paler and weaker than small swatches.

    Since then: measurements mostly find that a large area looks lighter, as Wada said, but more vivid, not weaker. Decorators often choose a wall paint a step greyer than its sample for this reason.

  • Time. Colours fade. Mineral pigments last, while plant dyes and coal-tar dyes change and fade far sooner, and white paper yellows in sunlight and air. Wada used only pigments that had passed a fading test.

    Since then: many synthetic pigments last as well as mineral ones, and paints now carry a lightfastness rating, pigment by pigment.

Strong and weak, warm and cool

Colours also carry feelings. Some excite more than others, some feel warm or cool, and many carry meanings.

  • Strength. Some colours excite the eye more than others. Wada put this down mostly to how strong a colour is, and partly to its hue. By hue, red excites most, then orange, yellow, green, blue and indigo, and violet least. People differ, he said, but this order is generally accepted, and red catches the eye so easily that it's used for danger signals. Strong combinations look splendid at first sight but tend to vulgarity and tire quickly, so they suit advertising, shop fronts and the stage better than the home (see Making a combination).

    Since then: studies find that how strong a colour is, its saturation and lightness, matters far more than its hue, as Wada's first point says, and no fixed order by hue holds. Red for danger is partly convention.

  • Warm and cool. Red, orange and yellow are warm, and blue and green cool. Hama traced it to the flames of a fire and the clear water of a deep pool. Every hue has warmer and cooler kinds, though. For Wada, celadon () is a warm green and green bamboo () a cool one. Warm colours suggest warmth and intimacy. Cool ones, to Hama, suggest coolness, purity and quiet, where Europeans and Americans call them cold and chilly.

  • What colours stand for. Hama listed meanings that colours carry, "a kind of code". White and black stand for sanctity, purity and seriousness, red for power and passion, yellow for light and joy, green for peace and health, blue for calm and depth, and purple for grace and nobility. But which colours people like depends on custom, age, education and surroundings, he added, so no absolute law can be laid down.

    Since then: some associations are widely shared, such as red with anger and with love, but many vary by culture. In Japan, white was long the colour of mourning.

Colour systems

A colour system orders colours by their attributes, so that any colour can be found and written down. This site places Wada's colours in four, each a view under Colors.

  • Ostwald. Every colour is a mix of full colour, white and black, so each hue is a triangle running from its full colour to white and to black, on a circle of 100 hues. Wada held Ostwald's theory to be "far above all others" and wrote colours in it, with 16 greys from white to black, lettered A to P, which this view follows. Hama drew the triangle with amounts of white and black of his own.

    Since then: Ostwald's system has fallen out of use, and Munsell's has become the standard. The Swedish NCS still describes colours much as Ostwald did, by how much white, black and full colour they seem to hold.

  • OKLCH. A modern colour space, of lightness, chroma and hue, made so that equal steps look roughly equal. The site computes every colour in it from the book's CMYK, and uses it to show them on screen.
  • Munsell. Hue, value (lightness) and chroma, spaced to look even, with chroma reaching as far as colours go. Japan's colour standards write colours in it.
  • PCCS. Made by the Japan Color Research Institute, which grew out of the association Wada founded. Its lightness follows Munsell's value, and it adds tones (colours of like lightness and saturation across the hues), for colour harmony.

Colour names

Wada gathered colour names into books, the Shikimei Sōkan and the Shikimei-chō, published by the Japan Standard Colour Association he founded, and gave every colour of his combinations a Japanese name and an English one. The English names come from Robert Ridgway's Color Standards and Color Nomenclature, a few misspelled, such as Antwarp Blue. Plate 8 of the 1935 atlas Wada made with Hama, Hyōjun shikisai zushū, shows 12 hues, each as tint, full colour and shade, with Wada's names. All 36 are colours here, 35 matched by name and one by our own reading of the plate, and their dialogs say so.

Hama adds notes on Japanese names. Ao long meant both green and blue. The browns (cha) and the mouse greys (nezumi) were the two great families of colour names in the Edo period, and both spread far. Cha, at first the colour of coarse tea, came to take in reds and even greens, such as bush-warbler brown, and nezumi took in blues and purples, such as wisteria grey. Kyoto's purple was redder than Edo's.

Wada Sanzō 和田三造

Wada Sanzō (和田三造, 1883–1967), a Japanese painter and designer, pioneered colour standards in Japan.

He trained in Western-style painting under Kuroda Seiki from 1899, and at the Tokyo School of Fine Arts from 1901 to 1904. In 1909 the Ministry of Education sent him to study in Europe: he travelled mainly in France, studying craft design as well as painting. On his way home, in 1914, he studied Eastern art in India and Burma. In 1915 he went back to Japan.

In 1927 he founded the Japan Standard Color Association, out of a concern that Japan needed standard colours.

From 1932 to 1944 he taught design at the Tokyo School of Fine Arts, as a professor.

His book, the Haishoku Sōkan (配色總鑑), he wrote, was not scientific: it came from his sense of colour as a painter, who had learned, painting from nature, to take apart its complex tones and put them together again. He also drew on European colour work: Ostwald's system to write colours down, Chevreul's rules for harmony, Ridgway's colour names.

His work on colour

Two parts of his work on colour lead to this site: a common reference for colours, and how to combine them well.

  • Standard colours. He made Japan's first systematic colour samples, and founded the association that became the Japan Color Research Institute, which went on to make the country's standard colour charts: the first in Japan with steps that look even, and the first chips anywhere of Munsell's revised system. It also helped draft JIS Z 8721, Japan's industrial standard for writing a colour down by its hue, lightness and saturation. To tie names to colours, his association put some 600 colour names onto 171 samples, each with its Japanese, Chinese and English names and its code in Ostwald's notation.
  • Colour combinations. The Haishoku Sōkan sets out 348 combinations of two, three and four colours, painted by hand, with his advice on using them (see Guidance). It is a reference for choosing colours that work together, in clothes, interiors, crafts or advertising. It was the first of the many books of colour combinations since.

This site

This site is built on Wada's colours and colour combinations. It shows his 171 colours and his combinations on screen, to browse, compare and use in your own work.

Wada's colours and combinations come from two books. Seigensha reprinted his Haishoku Sōkan as A Dictionary of Color Combinations, and a second volume adds his seasonal combinations.

Each colour is computed from the books' CMYK, using the Japan Color 2011 Coated profile, the standard for offset printing on coated paper in Japan.

The site also shows how Wada's palette sits in four colour systems:

  • Ostwald. Every colour as a mix of a full colour with white and black. The one Wada used in his work.
  • Munsell. Hue, value (lightness) and chroma, spaced so that the steps look even. The one Japan's colour standards use.
  • PCCS. Its lightness follows Munsell's value, and it groups colours into tones, of similar lightness and saturation across the hues, for colour harmony. Made by the institute that grew out of Wada's association.
  • OKLCH. A modern colour space of lightness, chroma and hue, made so that equal steps look about equally different.

The site has five main topics:

  • Colors. Wada's palette, grouped by colour family as in the book, and placed in four colour systems (see Colour theory).
  • Combinations. The 348 combinations of the first volume, laid out as in the book.
  • Seasons. The 72 seasonal combinations of the second volume, month by month.
  • Create. A small tool to create your own combinations, from Wada's colours or any colour of the four systems.
  • About. This page, plus the colour theory behind combinations (Colour theory) and Wada's and Hama Yaohiko's advice on making and using them (Guidance).
