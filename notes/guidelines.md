# Wada's guidelines

What Wada himself advised, from his 1935 supplement and his 1934 booklet. Page numbers are the supplement's unless marked 1934. The sources and links are in [README.md](README.md#sources).

## Quick reference

**Making a combination:**

1. **Start from one hue in its light and dark tones.** That is safe. Contrast different hues only with care.
2. **Balance lightness and saturation, not just hue:**
   - several vivid colours need a muted one among or beside them;
   - several pale colours need a measured amount of a deep one;
   - several dark colours need some lighter ones.
3. **Avoid equal strength everywhere.** If every colour is about as strong as the others, the result is safe but "ordinary".
4. **Where contrasting hues touch,** weigh each colour's lightness and strength, or the result turns coarse.
5. **Complementary pairs are lively** but suit fewer uses. [His table](#his-complementary-pairs-p-22) pairs colours from his own palette.
6. **Prefer calm tones for things you live with.** Strong colours suit advertising and display, and they tire quickly.
7. **If two colours clash,** put white, grey or black between them. [Chevreul's rules](#neutrals-and-outlines-chevreuls-rules-pp-2326) say which.

**Using a combination:**

1. **Equal areas only work when the colours are equally strong.** Otherwise, give weak tones the large areas and strong tones the small ones.
2. **The largest colour unifies the rest,** but the focal point can be a small accent.
3. **Large areas look paler** and weaker than the chips.
4. **Try the amounts** by laying cut white or grey paper over the plate.

## His stance

> This method of combining colours is not something to be taught in words, as we learn mathematics, where following a formula gives a correct answer. It always appeals to each person's own feelings and tastes, so to talk of a "method of combination" is something one can say but cannot really say. Still, as someone who has handled colour all along, I want to discuss in plain terms what I have experienced of colour contrast, colour change, and their relation to purpose, place and season. (pp. 3–4)

- **"Non-scientific" (1934, pp. 9–10, not in the reprint):**
  - He calls his book a "non-scientific organisation", which pure scientists will probably criticise.
  - It comes from his own sense of colour, as a painter who learned, painting from nature, to "separate and synthesise" its complex tones. So it differs from what a scientist would argue from the spectrum.
  - Ōmi makes the same point: unlike Munsell or Ostwald, Wada proposed no theory of harmony built on a colour order system.
- **No science of it yet (pp. 26–28):**
  - a strict scientific method may come one day, but so far colour combination can only be treated as subjective and emotional;
  - "however great the authority of colour science", it can't explain logically why a combination works;
  - what one has is experience and habit.
- **Taste is learnt (p. 28):** taste in colour (色の味, 色感) is like a singing voice. It is partly a gift, but it improves with practice.
- **The basis for practice:** he accepts Chevreul's rules, and adds his own rule on balance (below).

## Reading a colour

- **Three attributes (pp. 4–5):**
  - hue (色相);
  - luminosity (光度): how light or dark the colour is, which falls as it takes on black;
  - saturation (飽和度, also 色度).

  A colour's tones run from white, through the pure colour, to black.
- **Notation (pp. 8–14):**
  - **The system:** he explains Ostwald's notation: a number for the hue, then two letters for how much white and how much black the colour holds.
  - **His colour-name books** (色名總鑑 and 色名帖) give their colours in it. For example, scorched brown (焦茶色) is 17PK. Next to orange at 16PA, it is a little redder and much darker.
- **The 1934 version (1934, pp. 17–20, not in the reprint):**
  - **Hue (色相):** built on the three primaries blue, red and yellow, as in three-colour printing.
  - **Degree (色度):** how strong the colour is, compared with the pure spectral one. Red thinned with water is paler, and red layered on red is deeper. He says 濃淡 (deep and pale) means the same thing.
  - **Light and dark (明暗):** how much black the colour holds, like the black plate added in colour printing. Grey laid over a greenish yellow gives olive. He suggests names such as 灰赤色 (greyish red).
- **Strength (pp. 14–15):**
  - **The order:** red excites the eye most, then orange, yellow, green, blue and indigo, with violet least. People differ (some feel yellow as stronger than red), but this order is generally accepted.
  - **Why red gets used:** danger signals and neon advertising use red because it catches the eye.
- **Quality and material (1934):**
  - **Transparent or opaque, warm or cold:** warm colours are red, orange-yellow and yellow; cold ones are blue and green. Each has its own shades:
    - 鶸萌木 (siskin green) and 青磁 (celadon) are warm greens;
    - 青竹 (green bamboo) and 常磐 (evergreen) are cold ones;
    - 金糸雀 (canary) is a colder yellow than 鬱金 (turmeric) or 山吹 (kerria).
  - **The material changes the colour:**
    - the same colour differs as oil paint, watercolour or dye;
    - thinned with water it turns transparent, and mixed with white it turns opaque;
    - silk, cotton and linen dyed alike come out differently.
  - **Warm and cold** appear only here, as a quality of a colour, never as something to balance.

## Making a combination

### Balance lightness and saturation, not just hue (p. 29)

Wada gives this as the first thing to practise:

> The arrangement of the three elements of colour (hue, luminosity, saturation) is the basis. Lining up similar or different hues to make a same-tone or different-tone combination is fine, but that alone won't give a satisfying combination. The effect of a combination comes not from hue alone, but from how the luminosity and saturation of each hue approach and balance one another.

- **Vivid combinations:** "To get a beautiful, sharp combination, don't just line up the saturated colour of each hue: some colour of low chroma must be inserted among them or placed next to them."
- **Pale combinations:** "When using only weak, pale tones, lining up colours of the same strength gives only a lukewarm feeling, with no flavour: a deep colour, in a measured amount, must be fitted in."
- **Dark combinations:** "Lining up only dull browns, dull greens or dull indigos is suffocating and never works, however well the hues are chosen: some of them must be lightened to a relatively light tone."
- **The comparison:** "As in cooking, different ingredients alone aren't enough: a meal comes together through the balance of hot, sweet and sour."

### Calm tones over strong ones (pp. 14–16)

- **What strong colours do:** combinations of strong colours look splendid at first and catch the eye, but they are often called vulgar.
- **Where they belong:** advertising, commercial design, stage sets and shop windows, where catching attention is the point.
- **Why to be wary:** few strong combinations last. We tire of them quickly, as of anything striking.
- **Choose calm (穏当) tones.** "Calm" doesn't mean desaturated. It means "neighbouring hues, with their strengths well balanced, so that the whole is calm".

### Four kinds of harmony (pp. 18–23)

1. **Analogous (類似色調和):**
   - **What it is:** one base hue across its tones, light and dark alike. With orange as the base:
     - the dark tones are kite brown (鳶茶), chestnut (栗梅), bulrush (蒲色), ochre (代赭), tobacco (煙草色) and clove brown (丁字茶);
     - the light ones are gardenia (深支子), incense (香色), camel (駱駝色) and flesh (肌色).
   - **How it behaves:** it is the most common kind, and gentle, because the gradation has no sudden jumps. "Whoever handles it, there is no fear of a great failure."
   - **Its limit:** "it cannot give an especially interesting, excellent harmony: only rather ordinary ones".
   - **Where to start:** amateurs should begin here, rather than go straight for different hues and fail.
2. **Different hues (異色調和):**
   - **What it is:** colours of entirely different hue families set against one another. With red as the base, that means orange, yellow, green, blue and purple, in any tone.
   - **Warning:** "Because the hues that touch are opposed, unless you combine them thinking carefully about the feeling and character of each colour's lightness and strength, the result often turns coarse and vulgar."
3. **Complementary (補色調和):**
   - **What it is:** opposite hues, whatever their lightness. It is part of the different-hues kind, but stronger, and much debated between scholars and practitioners.
   - **How it behaves:** used well, it narrows where a combination can be used, but gives "lively, excellent" ones.
   - **How it works:** he explains it by after-images. Stare at red paper on white, take it away, and green appears; yellow leaves ultramarine, and purple leaves green-yellow.
4. **Graded bands (暈繝法調和, *ungen*):**
   - **What it is:** a single hue in steps. The saturated colour sits in the middle, with lighter tones stepping to white on one side and darker tones to black on the other. A combination takes part of that scale.
   - **How it behaves:** calmer still than the others.

#### His complementary pairs (p. 22)

| Colour | Complement |
|---|---|
| Spectrum Red | Sea Green |
| Red Orange | Green Blue (a slightly greener one) |
| Orange | Peacock Blue |
| Yellow Orange | Antwerp Blue |
| Apricot Yellow | Blue |
| Yellow | Deep Lyons Blue |
| Lemon Yellow | Violet Ultramarine (only in Vol 2) |
| Light Green Yellow | Violet |
| Yellow Green | Red Violet |
| Night Green | Rosolane Purple |

- **How exact it is:** he made the table from his palette's colours, and calls it "close to complete". His colours weren't mixed to be complements, so their lightness and saturation vary. Only the hue matters.
- **Light and dark tones count:** Sea Green (若竹色) is weaker than red, yet still its complement. So are red's light tone (鴇色, pink) and its dark tone (海老茶, maroon).
- **In the book:** only one of these pairs appears together, Yellow with Deep Lyons Blue in #22. Chance would give about one too ([data](data-analysis.md#wadas-rules-in-the-plates)), so he neither sought these pairs nor avoided them.

### Neutrals and outlines: Chevreul's rules (pp. 23–26)

Wada presents these as an excerpt from Chevreul, "the first person in the study of colour contrast and harmony". He gives Chevreul's dates as 1786–1879; Chevreul actually died in 1889.

- **Contrast pairs:** complementary pairs, such as green with red or ultramarine with yellow, give good harmony by contrast.
- **Primaries:**
  - two of the primaries (red, yellow and blue) harmonise better than a primary with a secondary colour that contains it;
  - a primary with such a secondary harmonises well when the primary has more brilliance.
- **Separating colours:** if two colours clash, white between them eases it. More generally, white, grey or black between them can help, but mind their saturation, lightness and luminosity.
- **Black:**
  - it never clashes with two bright colours;
  - it mostly goes well with dark purples and blues;
  - with one bright and one dark colour, it is less pleasing than with two bright ones.
- **Grey:**
  - it goes well with two bright colours, and never clashes even with two dull ones;
  - with dull blues or purples, black works better than grey;
  - with a bright and a dark colour, white works better than grey, and when both cover large areas, black can be used instead.
- **Which neutral to put between two colours:**
  - for red and yellow, anything but black;
  - for red and blue, anything but white;
  - for blue and yellow, anything but grey;
  - for yellow and orange, anything but black;
  - for blue and purple, anything but grey;
  - black suits orange with green, grey suits purples, and white suits purple with green.
- **Outlines in a design (p. 26):**
  - on a pale ground, draw letters and patterns in deep tones, outlined in black, grey or a colour deeper than the pattern;
  - a pattern paler than its ground takes a white or grey outline;
  - a one-colour pattern built from light and dark steps takes a white or grey outline on a deep ground, and a deep outline on a pale one.
- **Material:** colours on glossy materials such as glass or silk harmonise better than on matte ones such as cotton or wool. Two glossy colours together look better still.

**His caveat (pp. 26–28):**

- Chevreul's rules are carefully considered, and many are still worth following. But their scientific basis is weak, so they can't settle everything.
- Chevreul has been dead for over fifty years, and taste moves on.
- By the rules, blue with red is pleasant, yet "apart from primitive people and children, few people with a little thought would agree".
- Brown next to black was long frowned on, yet it is now used with the greatest pleasure.
- A colour always shows itself through contrast with what surrounds it, so what pleased one age need not please the next.

### The 1934 scheme: same tone and different tone (1934, pp. 21–25, also in the reprint)

This is the "2 viewpoints, 10 patterns" that Ōmi calls the core of Wada's method. It is an earlier, coarser form of the 1935 kinds of harmony.

- **同色調, same tone:** harmony by gradual change within one family of colours. He calls it "modulation" (his English word) and compares it with music.
- **異色調, different tone:** harmony sought between colours of entirely different quality. He calls it the hardest kind and the most interesting. Nature shows it often, he says: things don't harmonise only by raising or lowering intensity.

Both kinds come in five tones:

| Tone | Same tone | Different tone | Wada's description |
|---|---|---|---|
| 明, light | 明同色調 | 明異色調 | bright, of the same intensity |
| 暗, dark | 暗同色調 | 暗異色調 | dark, yet of a somewhat similar tone |
| 淡, pale | 淡同色調 | 淡異色調 | neighbouring colours of equal tone, at weakened intensity |
| 濃又深, rich or deep | 濃又深同色調 | 濃又深異色調 | strongly stimulating, rather primitive, rich and deep colours fused together (he points to the textiles and buildings of "primitive peoples") |
| 汎, pan | 汎同色調 | 汎異色調 | from the Greek *pan*: tones that pass freely into one another, combining light, dark, deep and pale at will |

- **"Same colour" means the same family,** not the same colour. His example of a same-tone kimono outfit:
  - a sky-blue haori;
  - a kimono dyed with a touch of ultramarine;
  - an obi greener than sky blue;
  - a yellowish-green collar.
- **Which to choose:** for clothes, interiors and furniture, 汎同色調 (pan, same tone) is the safe choice, and easy for anyone. 異色調 is very hard, but more rewarding.
- **The tables are a guide, not rules:**
  - He calls them "a canon, a standard, a pillar", but you need not obey them. You may merge them or break them, and forcing nature into such categories is rigid.
  - Making excellent new combinations that way takes great experience of how much of a colour to use (色量) and of its quality (色質).
  - What he shows is "a very ordinary arrangement of tones", drawn from his long experience and from the theories of various authorities. If it helps people make good combinations, he says, his aim is fulfilled.
- **Composition (構成) comes in two kinds:**
  - traditional or customary: practical, but ordinary;
  - individually created: novel and surprising.

  A standard is a model, never something to copy.
- **Nature is the model:** it harmonises everywhere and never breaks harmony. Man-made combinations are completed by following it and learning from its harmonies.
- **The plates leave the reading to you:** none says which of the ten types it is. As Ōmi puts it, Wada doesn't lecture on rules to follow, but lets the colours speak: "read the colours".

## Using a combination

### Area follows strength (pp. 32–33)

- **The plates aren't balanced for equal areas.** Many of them combine colours far apart in strength: not all are even in saturation, lightness and darkness.
- **The principle:** colours shown at equal areas should be of equal strength. More precisely, harmony comes only when the chromatic colours (such as red, blue and yellow) balance out, and so do light and dark, that is, the amounts of white and black.
- **Real use:** this is only theory, he adds. Equal areas are very rare, and the strengths usually vary too.
- **So:** "put weak tones on large areas and strong tones on small ones".
- **How to try it:** cut white or grey paper into shapes and lay it over a plate, to see a strong colour at a small area.

**The 1934 booklet's last chapter,** "Amount of colour and combination (how to use the Haishoku Sōkan)", says the same at more length:

- **Equal areas are only a convenience:** every chip in the book is the same size. In real clothes and designs, colours of equal amount and shape are extremely rare, and better avoided.
- **The helper cards:** the book came with cut-out cards (配色補助カード): a triangle, a rectangle and a wavy band, reproduced in the reprint. Lay one over a plate to change the shape and amount of each colour. He calls this "the shortest way" to good combinations.
- **A kimono example:**
  - the han-eri (collar) is a tiny amount, the kimono and haori take most, and the obi comes next;
  - lay the triangle over a 3-colour plate: its tip is the han-eri, the middle chip is the kimono, and the next is the obi or haori.
- **More colours:** cut your own cards, and treat the plate's mount paper as a white chip.
- **No ratio:** he sets none. The proportions are for the user to try.

### The main colour and the focal point (p. 34)

- **On the B篇 cards,** the colour with the largest area is at the top, as the central colour ("though there are exceptions"). Looking at a card, that colour is "the main colour (主色), the so-called dominant colour (支配色)", thought of as unifying all the others. In our data the top band is the largest on all 72 cards.
- **In actual dress,** a small area often becomes the main colour, the centre of the decoration that unifies the rest:
  - in kimono: a shawl, the obi, the han-eri or an obi clip, rather than the large haori or kimono;
  - in Western dress: a hat, shoes, a necklace, gloves, the folds or a handbag, rather than the dress.
- "It is a question of where the centre of the combination, the centre of the decoration, is."

### Large areas look paler (p. 34; 1934)

- **The effect:** a combination that works in small chips looks paler and weaker on a large object, even in the same tones.
- **The example:** clothes ordered from small swatches come out "surprisingly washed out", so that you suspect they aren't the same cloth.
- **His advice:** "colourists must take great note of this".

## Wada reads two of his own cards (pp. 30–31)

**January, card 1:**

- **The colours:** flesh colour (肌色, our Seashell Pink) as the main colour, with white (白色, White), 蒸栗色 (Sulphur Yellow), 裏葉色 (Glaucous Green) and 薄色 (Grayish Lavender).
- **What's wrong with it:** all the colours are light. Apart from the white they are about equal in strength, with no great difference in hue, lightness or saturation. "No colour is strong or weak; none helps or sets off another; each just asserts itself", so it has no character and no change.
- **His verdict:** in one word, ordinary (平凡). Its one consolation is that it is safe. This kind is very common: the most typical of what anyone can make.
- **His reservation:** this is an objective view, and in practical use it may be read differently.

**January, card 5:**

- **The colours:** black as the main colour, with red (赤色, Spectrum Red), 白茶 (Ivory Buff), 深縹 (Dark Tyrian Blue), 青磁色 (Cobalt Green) and grey (灰色, Warm Gray).
- **The contrast with card 1:** it differs in every way. Hue, lightness and saturation are completely different, and the strengths are uneven. Yet it doesn't look unnatural: "at first sight it gives a powerful feeling, full of change". His reasons, colour by colour:
  - **Black:** it is passive next to white's active nature, and hard to call neutral. But as the darkest of colours, it suits a strong unity.
  - **The red:** a near-spectral red, a pure colour with neither white nor black in it. Seen alone, such a red often turns coarse and vulgar. Here black's great embrace and the contrast of the Ivory Buff below soften it, so it looks excellent "in a good sense".
  - **The Dark Tyrian Blue,** under the Ivory Buff, is there for the overall harmony. When a relatively weak tone such as the Cobalt Green sits below, you need a colour of quite different hue or lightness, or a hue like this deep blue, to tighten the whole.
  - **The grey, last:** partly because of the black at the top, but mainly because, by contrast, it changes none of the other colours.
- **Comparing the two cards:**
  - In card 1 everything is in the same tone, so it is hard to tell which colour is the main one and which are supporting (補助色).
  - In card 5 black covers the large area as the main colour, yet the colour that catches everyone's eye first is the red.
  - "So in use, the black with its large area is not necessarily the subject of the decoration": here the centre must be the red.

## Background: nature, peoples and periods (1934, chapters 1–4, not in the reprint)

- **Colour sense follows land and light:** the red-ochre (紅殻) and palm-coloured clothes of India suit its land, and French colours suit France's soft sky. He speaks of "national colours", and of Paris shop windows with refined combinations.
- **Pure colours versus refined ones:**
  - "Primitive" peoples build on single, pure colours; cultured ones prefer compound colours and refined tones beyond the primaries.
  - A combination is a "second nature" made by people, and the effort to find harmony in it goes with the level of culture.
- **A period shows in its textiles:**
  - in Japan: the Heian, Ashikaga, Momoyama and Tokugawa periods, the subdued (渋い) tones of the Keichō era and the splendour of Genroku;
  - in France: the cream and pale crimson of Louis XVI.
- **Earlier work on harmony:** Chevreul's harmony tables were admired in their day but went out of date as taste moved on, which Wada calls natural. He also cites Cennino Cennini's *Libro dell'arte*.
