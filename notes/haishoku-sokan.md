# The Haishoku Sōkan

What Wada made, why, and in which editions. The sources are listed in [README.md](README.md#sources).

## Wada and why he made it

- **Who he was:** Wada (1883–1967) was a Western-style painter and a pupil of Kuroda Seiki. He became professor of design at the Tokyo School of Fine Arts in 1932. Later he won an Academy Award for the costumes of *Gate of Hell* (地獄門) (essay).
- **Where his colour work began:** in India, a trading-company man told him how hard it was to agree on colours in trade without standard samples. He went on to found the Japan Standard Color Association (1927), which became the Japan Color Research Institute in 1945 (essay).
- **Three lines of work:** Ōmi sorts Wada's colour work into three:
  - colour names (色名總鑑 1931, 色名帖 1932);
  - colour order systems (色の標準 1951, the first perceptually uniform system made in Japan);
  - colour harmony, where the Haishoku Sōkan belongs.
- **His preface (發刊の辭, 1934):**
  - words like blue, red, cream or rose can't record a combination;
  - the existing references are too big and unwieldy for amateurs;
  - taste is subjective, yet a standard list of combinations can be "an easy everyday adviser on colour, and to some extent a decisive guide";
  - clothes, furniture and street scenery are all becoming colourful.

  His readers are everyone, not one trade.
- **Who it was for (1935):**
  - the A篇: arts and crafts, clothing design, architecture, interiors, furniture, shop windows and advertising (p. 32);
  - the B篇: research material for designers, craftsmen and dyers, and above all for people working on Japanese and Western clothing (p. 34).
- **The names:** each colour has a Japanese name and an English one. The English names come from Ridgway's *Color Standards and Color Nomenclature* (1912), a few of them misspelled (see `vol2/README.md`).

## Editions

| Edition | Years | Form | Combinations |
|---|---|---|---|
| First edition (配色總鑑 第1–第6) | 1933–34 | six bound volumes, two plates to a page | 120 two-colour, 120 three-colour and 108 four-colour: 348 |
| Card edition (A篇, A.I–A.XII) | 1934–35 | twelve boxes of 30 loose cards | 120 of each kind: 360 |
| B篇 | 1934–35 | 12 monthly volumes of 6 cards | 72 seasonal combinations |

- **First edition:**
  - **Contents:** volumes 1–2 hold the two-colour combinations, 3–4 the three-colour and 5–6 the four-colour. Volume 6 ends with plates (107) and (108), followed by the colophon.
  - **How it was sold:** by subscription, in instalments, and not in order. The publisher's notice in the 1934 booklet reads: "4th delivery: volume 5, four-colour combinations (part 1)… next delivery: volume 4, three-colour combinations (part 2)". The booklet came with that 4th delivery (February 1934), along with a sample helper card (配色補助カード, see [guidelines.md](guidelines.md#area-follows-strength-pp-3233)).
  - **The plates:** larger than the reprint's. Strip samples of the colours were sold separately (essay).
  - **The reprint:** Ōmi's essay describes this edition, and the reprint reproduces it, numbered as in the original (1–120, 121–240, 241–348). It drops the original's supplementary colour names, and adds CMYK values in its index (凡例).
- **Card edition:**
  - **Contents:** A.I–IV hold the two-colour cards, A.V–VIII the three-colour and A.IX–XII the four-colour. Each card is followed by its blank back.
  - **Numbering:** it restarts with each kind. Card (n) is our #n for two colours, #120+n for three and #240+n for four.
  - **Matching our data:**
    - A.VI ends with three-colour (59) and (60), our #179 and #180;
    - A.XII runs from four-colour (91), our #331, to (120);
    - its (108) matches our #348 exactly, and (109)–(120) are not in the reprint.
  - **Why cards (p. 32):** many combinations on one sheet keep each from showing its value, and loose cards are easier to compare and use. Enthusiasts had asked for them.
- **B篇:** each card is a strip, with one large colour on top and smaller bands below. The strips can be fanned out and moved, so users can try other combinations. Wada says this improves on a trial in a European magazine (p. 33). See `vol2/README.md`.
- **The reprint's Vol 2:** besides the B篇's 72 cards, it has 161 examples of colour in use (165 in one listing): clothing, interiors and graphic design of the early Shōwa period. Library records (CiNii) give their source as a 1937 design collection by Wada, 五百選新作図案集. I haven't checked this against the book.
- **Another record:** the NDL also has an A篇 in 12 parts (第1–12輯, 1934–35), e.g. https://dl.ndl.go.jp/pid/1145853. I haven't checked it plate by plate.

## What a card shows

- **On each card:**
  - a number;
  - the swatches;
  - each colour's Japanese name with its reading, and an English name.
- **An example:** plate 3 is 淡卵色 "Pale Lemon Yellow" and 檜皮色 "Raw Sienna", the same colours in the same order as our data.
- **Positions:** the four-colour cards mark them 上 (top), 左 (left), 右 (right) and 下 (bottom), confirming our order.
- **Layout:** a cross is four equal rectangles. Nothing on a card says what kind of harmony it is.
- **Why equal areas (p. 33):** Wada says real uses rarely have equal areas. But varying the sizes would narrow the range of uses, and equal chips were easier to make.

## The swatches

- **The papers:** hand-painted matte papers, pasted in (essay).
- **The pigments (p. 35):**
  - "all imported from first-class paint makers in Europe and America";
  - each tested for fading, though not guaranteed fast;
  - keep the cards out of direct sunlight, in a dry, dark place.
